Surrender your LIC policy or continue it?

"Should I close this policy and take the money?" It is a question I hear often, usually when money is tight or when someone has been told the returns are poor. Sometimes surrender is the right answer. But often it is a decision people regret, because they get back much less than they expected. Let's look at it calmly.

What does surrender mean?

Surrendering means ending the policy before its maturity date and taking whatever cash value it has at that moment. This is called the surrender value. Once you surrender, the policy is closed for good: there is no more life cover, no more bonuses, and it cannot be revived.

How is surrender value worked out?

A policy usually acquires a surrender value only after premiums have been paid for a minimum period stated in the policy, often two or three years. After that, LIC generally calculates two values:

  • Guaranteed surrender value (GSV): a percentage of the premiums paid, as set out in the policy document. The percentage is low in the early years and increases as the policy gets older.
  • Special surrender value (SSV): based on the paid-up value and vested bonuses, using factors decided by LIC.

You are normally paid the higher of the two. The exact method for your plan is in the "Conditions and privileges" section of your bond. Newer policies sold under recent IRDAI rules may have different surrender terms from older ones.

Why early surrender costs so much

In the first several years, a large part of what you have paid has gone toward life cover, expenses and building the policy. So the surrender value in the early years can be much less than the total premiums you have paid. In the later years, the gap narrows. This means the same decision can be costly at year 4 and quite reasonable at year 17.

There is also a tax point: if a policy ends within the first two years, any tax deductions you claimed on its premiums may be added back to your income.

Before you surrender, consider these options

  1. Take a policy loan. If you need cash for a short period, a loan against the policy gives you money while keeping the cover and bonuses running.
  2. Make it paid-up. If you simply can't pay premiums any more, stop paying and let the policy continue with a reduced sum assured until maturity. You often get more this way than by surrendering early.
  3. Change the premium mode. If a big yearly premium is the problem, ask whether a half-yearly, quarterly or monthly mode is possible for your policy.
  4. Wait for the maturity date. If maturity is only a few years away, continuing usually gives a much better result.

When surrender may make sense

  • You have a genuine emergency and no other option, including a loan.
  • You bought a policy that clearly doesn't fit your needs, the premium is a heavy burden, and you have proper life cover elsewhere, such as a term plan.
  • The policy is close to maturity anyway and you need the money now.

Whatever you decide, never surrender a policy before you have other life cover in place. If you are older or have developed a health condition, buying new cover later may be expensive or difficult.

A simple checklist

  1. Get the current surrender value and paid-up value in writing from LIC.
  2. Compare it with the total premiums paid and what you would get at maturity.
  3. Ask how much loan is available.
  4. Check you will still have enough life cover after surrender.
  5. Only then decide.

To surrender, you generally submit a surrender form, the original policy bond, KYC documents and bank details at the servicing branch. The money is paid to your bank account.
